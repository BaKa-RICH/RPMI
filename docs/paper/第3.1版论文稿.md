# Recovery-Debt-Constrained Perishable Merge-Slot Inventory Production and Reservation Control for Mixed Multi-Lane On-Ramp Merging

**Chinese title**: 面向恢复债务约束的易失合流槽库存生产与预约控制：混合多车道匝道合流的反事实边际价值方法  
**Method acronym**: RPMI-CMV, Recoverable Perishable Merge-Slot Inventory Control with Counterfactual Marginal Value  
**Version**: 3.1 research manuscript draft  

---

## Abstract

In mixed multi-lane on-ramp merging, the bottleneck is not simply the number of geometric gaps on the outer mainline lane. A gap is useful only if it is reachable by a specific ramp vehicle, remains available until execution, satisfies physical safety margins under short-horizon uncertainty, and does not induce excessive post-merge recovery burden on the mainline. Existing studies have extensively addressed cooperative merging, proactive gap creation, ramp-vehicle platooning, multi-lane lane-changing coordination, dynamic conflict graphs, and learning-based merging policies. This paper therefore does not claim gap creation or speed guidance as standalone novelties. Instead, it focuses on a narrower mechanism: how to measure, produce, reserve, consume, and reassign recoverable perishable merge-slot inventory.

We propose RPMI-CMV, a recovery-debt-constrained perishable merge-slot inventory control method with counterfactual marginal value. Candidate merge slots are represented as time-expanded resources $s=(i,j,k)$, where $(i,j)$ denotes the front and rear boundary vehicles on the target lane and $t_s=\tau_k$ is a candidate merge time generated before matching. For each ramp vehicle--slot edge $e=(r,i,j,k)$, we evaluate reachability, predicted physical validity, survival, recoverability, and recovery debt. The recoverable valid probability $P_e^R$ is expressed by a standard conditional probability chain for sequential slot-validity events rather than by an independence assumption. Effective inventory supply $S_H^R$ is defined as a matching-limited expected satisfied demand and compared with horizon demand $D_H$ to obtain the recoverable inventory deficit $Z_H^R$.

Version 3.1 deliberately simplifies the previous draft. Instead of treating all possible lane changes and CAV speed adjustments as a large multi-vehicle action scheduling problem, it adopts a gap-centric but ramp-aware production logic. A baseline diagnostic matching first determines whether natural recoverable inventory is sufficient. If not, the algorithm identifies near-miss producible edges: reachable ramp-vehicle--gap--time candidates that are currently invalid or low-quality but can plausibly become recoverable with a small mainline CAV action. Candidate production actions include upstream CAV lane change, front-boundary CAV acceleration, rear-boundary CAV deceleration, and front-rear joint micro-adjustment. Each action is evaluated through action-conditioned rollout and a new reservation problem; the selected action maximizes normalized counterfactual marginal value, balancing reduction in inventory deficit, demand-normalized recovery debt, and mainline disturbance cost.

The first validation stage is a transparent Python numerical simulation rather than SUMO. The simulation uses no hidden emergency controller, no simulator safety fallback, and no automatic repair of unsafe trajectories. Physical validity is declared before reservation and realized invalidity is recorded after rollout. The initial experiments prioritize deterministic mechanism verification, then stochastic IDM-based HDV uncertainty. The main designed scenario is not sparse traffic or all-lanes congestion, but a near-critical outer-lane condition where inner lanes retain reserve capacity, ramp demand is medium-high, and CAV penetration is sufficient for limited low-disturbance slot production.

**Keywords**: on-ramp merging; mixed traffic; connected and automated vehicles; perishable slot inventory; recovery debt; counterfactual marginal value; action-conditioned reservation; stochastic IDM; Python numerical simulation.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Freeway on-ramp merging is a recurrent bottleneck because ramp vehicles must enter the mainline within a limited acceleration and merging distance, while mainline vehicles experience cut-ins, local braking, lane changes, and speed-wave propagation. In mixed traffic, connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) can be guided or coordinated, whereas human-driven vehicles (HDVs) remain uncertain and can only be predicted. The problem is therefore not merely a deterministic ordering or trajectory-planning problem. It involves short-horizon prediction, competition among ramp vehicles for limited merging opportunities, cross-lane coupling, HDV uncertainty, and post-merge recovery.

A large body of work already studies CAV merging coordination, proactive gap creation, lane-changing assistance, dynamic conflict graphs, and learning-based policies. These studies make it inappropriate for a new paper to claim that it is the first to create gaps, guide ramp vehicles, or jointly consider mainline lane change and ramp merging. The unresolved mechanism addressed here is more specific: a raw geometric gap is not the same as a useful merge slot.

A gap may be geometrically large but still useless because:

1. the specific ramp vehicle cannot reach it at the right time;
2. the gap may expire before execution because of HDV fluctuations or speed-wave compression;
3. the gap may require unsafe or physically invalid insertion;
4. the post-merge disturbance may impose large braking, long recovery time, or local speed-wave amplification;
5. the gap may compete with other ramp vehicles or time-expanded copies of the same physical gap.

The control object should therefore be changed from raw gap count to **recoverable perishable merge-slot inventory**.

### 1.2 Core idea

The proposed method views the outer-lane merging opportunities within a finite horizon as an inventory resource with five properties.

First, the inventory is **pair-specific**. A slot is useful only for a ramp vehicle that can reach it under acceleration, jerk, speed, and ramp-end constraints.

Second, the inventory is **perishable**. A slot may disappear before it is consumed.

Third, the inventory is **recoverable or debt-bearing**. A merge may be collision-free but still impose high recovery burden on the mainline.

Fourth, the inventory is **matching-limited**. One slot is consumed by at most one ramp vehicle, and one ramp vehicle reserves at most one slot.

Fifth, the inventory is **producible but not free**. CAV lane changes and boundary speed micro-adjustments may improve a future slot, but they also disturb mainline traffic.

The method is summarized as:

$$
\text{perishable recoverable slot inventory}
+\text{near-miss slot production}
+\text{action-conditioned rolling reservation}.
$$

The most important refinement in Version 3.1 is that slot production is not formulated as a broad multi-CAV joint scheduling problem. Such a formulation would be theoretically possible, but it would shift the paper away from the inventory mechanism and toward a complex joint trajectory optimization problem. The main version instead uses a simpler and more implementable rule:

> Slot production is triggered only when a near-miss gap can be converted into a reachable and recoverable slot with sufficiently small mainline disturbance, and when the resulting action-conditioned reservation reduces the recoverable inventory deficit.

Thus the algorithm is **gap-centric but ramp-aware**: it searches for low-disturbance producible gaps that can serve ramp demand, not for arbitrary actions that serve a ramp vehicle at any cost.

### 1.3 Contributions

This paper makes three focused contributions.

**Contribution 1: Time-expanded recoverable perishable merge-slot inventory.**  
Candidate merge slots are defined as $s=(i,j,k)$, where $\tau_k$ is generated before matching. For each edge $e=(r,i,j,k)$, the method evaluates reachability, predicted physical validity, survival, safety, recoverability, and recovery debt. The conditional probability chain used in $P_e^R$ is not claimed as a probability-theory innovation; it is a standard product-rule expression of sequential slot-validity events.

**Contribution 2: Matching-consistent inventory deficit with conflict awareness.**  
The effective inventory supply $S_H^R$ is defined in the same unit as demand $D_H$, namely expected satisfied weighted ramp demand. Version 3.1 additionally recognizes that time-expanded copies of the same physical gap are not independent resources. A base model is a bipartite matching; a conflict-aware model adds slot-consumption constraints and is solved as a small rolling integer assignment.

**Contribution 3: RCMV-based near-miss slot production and action-conditioned reservation.**  
Candidate actions are generated from near-miss producible edges rather than from all mainline gaps. Upstream lane change, front-boundary acceleration, rear-boundary deceleration, and front-rear joint micro-adjustment are compared by the same counterfactual marginal value. After an action is selected, slots and reservations are recomputed under the action-conditioned rollout.

The no-fallback Python simulation is treated as a validation strategy, not as a methodological contribution.

## 2. Related Work and Positioning

### 2.1 Cooperative merging and proactive gap creation

Cooperative merging has been studied through vehicle sequencing, trajectory planning, longitudinal speed guidance, platoon formation, and proactive gap creation. Flow-level coordination methods integrate mainline lane-change rules, proactive creation of large merging gaps, and ramp-vehicle platooning to improve multilane ramp-merging efficiency. DCoMA-style strategies further combine macro-level gap creation with microscopic ramp-vehicle control under mixed traffic.

These studies motivate RPMI-CMV but also define its boundary. The present paper does not claim that proactive gap creation is new. Its claim is that a created gap should be evaluated as a pair-specific, perishable, recoverable, and matching-limited inventory item before being counted as effective supply.

### 2.2 Multi-lane lane changing and dynamic conflict graphs

Dynamic conflict graph methods formulate multi-lane on-ramp merging as a unified problem involving ramp merging and mainline lane-changing conflicts. Such methods are powerful but often become broad joint decision-making frameworks. RPMI-CMV differs by using lane change as only one possible slot-production primitive. A lane change is not valuable because it improves the CAV's own speed or balances density; it is valuable only if, under action-conditioned rollout, it improves recoverable inventory and justifies its disturbance cost.

### 2.3 HDV modeling and lane-changing uncertainty

The Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) is a classical continuous car-following model with interpretable parameters such as desired speed, desired time headway, minimum gap, maximum acceleration, and comfortable deceleration. MOBIL derives lane-changing decisions from acceleration incentives, safety, and politeness. For the first mechanism validation, this paper adopts stochastic IDM for HDV longitudinal behavior and does not model active HDV lane changing. MOBIL, cut-ins, and HDV lane-change uncertainty are reserved for robustness experiments and journal extensions.

This choice is intentional. If HDV lane changing and random cut-ins are introduced in the first experiment, the effect of perishable inventory and RCMV-based production becomes difficult to isolate.

### 2.4 Learning-based merging control

Deep reinforcement learning and graph-based multi-agent reinforcement learning have been used for ramp-merging decisions, desired merging position selection, cooperative merging, and multi-lane coordination. These methods are important for future comparison, but they are not the main baseline in Version 3.1. The present paper is mechanism-driven rather than learning-driven. Learning may later be used to approximate $P^R$, $RD$, or $RCMV$, while exact rollout remains the verifier for top-ranked actions.

### 2.5 Simulation platform choice

SUMO is a mature microscopic traffic simulator with rich car-following and lane-changing logic. Its documentation also makes clear that safety checks, speed modes, and lane-change modes can affect whether collisions or unsafe maneuvers occur. This makes SUMO valuable for external validation but less suitable as the first tool for mechanism verification. RPMI-CMV depends on counterfactual rollout and explicit recording of invalid candidates. A transparent Python numerical simulator is therefore used first, followed by SUMO or calibrated microscopic simulation later.

## 3. Problem Setting

### 3.1 Road geometry and vehicle sets

Consider a multi-lane freeway on-ramp merging area. The outer mainline lane adjacent to the ramp is denoted by $\ell_o$, and the inner lanes are denoted by $\mathcal L_i$. Ramp vehicles must merge into $\ell_o$ before the ramp end. The prediction horizon is $[t,t+H]$, the control update interval is $\Delta t$, and the candidate merge-time interval is $\Delta t_m$.

The vehicle set is

$$
\mathcal V_t=\mathcal M_t\cup\mathcal R_t,
$$

where $\mathcal M_t$ is the set of mainline vehicles and $\mathcal R_t$ is the set of ramp vehicles. Each vehicle has type

$$
\tau_n\in\{\mathrm{CAV},\mathrm{HDV}\}.
$$

The state of vehicle $n$ is

$$
X_n(t)=\big(x_n(t),v_n(t),u_n(t),\ell_n(t),L_n,\tau_n\big).
$$

Here $x_n(t)$ is the front-bumper longitudinal position, $v_n(t)$ is speed, $u_n(t)$ is longitudinal acceleration, $\ell_n(t)$ is lane index, and $L_n$ is vehicle length. Vehicle $n$ occupies the interval

$$
[x_n(t)-L_n,\ x_n(t)].
$$

If vehicle $i$ is ahead of vehicle $j$ on the same lane, their net bumper-to-bumper gap is

$$
g_{ij}(\tau)=x_i(\tau)-L_i-x_j(\tau).
$$

### 3.2 Vehicle behavior model

#### HDV longitudinal behavior

An HDV $h$ follows stochastic IDM:

$$
\dot x_h=v_h,
\qquad
\dot v_h=u_h^{HDV},
$$

$$
u_h^{HDV}(\tau)=f_{IDM}(X_\tau;\theta_h)+\eta_h(\tau),
$$

where $\eta_h$ is bounded or truncated zero-mean acceleration noise. The deterministic IDM term is

$$
f_{IDM}=a_h\left[1-\left(\frac{v_h}{v_{0,h}}\right)^{\delta_h}
-\left(\frac{s_h^*(v_h,\Delta v_h)}{s_h}\right)^2\right],
$$

$$
s_h^*=s_{0,h}+v_hT_h+\frac{v_h\Delta v_h}{2\sqrt{a_hb_h}}.
$$

The parameters are desired speed $v_0$, desired time headway $T$, minimum gap $s_0$, maximum acceleration $a$, comfortable deceleration $b$, and exponent $\delta$. Driver heterogeneity is represented by conservative, normal, and aggressive parameter classes. The first deterministic experiment sets $\eta_h=0$; stochastic experiments use $\eta_h\ne0$.

HDV active lane changing is not included in the initial Python experiment.

#### CAV longitudinal model with vehicle length

CAVs use a traffic-layer longitudinal point-mass model:

$$
\dot x_c=v_c,
\qquad
\dot v_c=u_c,
$$

with constraints

$$
u_c^{min}\le u_c\le u_c^{max},
\qquad
|\dot u_c|\le j_c^{max},
\qquad
0\le v_c\le v^{max}.
$$

The model is not a full vehicle dynamics model. It is a longitudinal traffic model with explicit vehicle length, sufficient for gap geometry, reachability, physical validity declaration, and local disturbance measurement.

#### Ramp CAV speed guidance

Ramp CAV speed guidance is treated as slot consumption, not as slot production. Given a candidate time $\tau_k$ and a feasible merge interval $\mathcal I_{r,i,j,k}(\tau_k)$, ramp vehicle $r$ is reachable if there exists a control trajectory $u_r(\cdot)$ such that

$$
x_r(\tau_k)\in\mathcal I_{r,i,j,k}(\tau_k)
$$

under acceleration, jerk, speed, and ramp-end constraints. The magnitude of ramp guidance can enter a secondary reservation cost. It is not counted as mainline slot-production value unless an explicit joint mainline-ramp action is evaluated.

### 3.3 Prediction horizon

The horizon $H$ should cover two processes that occur largely in parallel: ramp-vehicle arrival and mainline slot-production effect. A practical design rule is

$$
H\ge H_{min}
=\max\left\{\max_{r\in\mathcal R_H}T_r^{reach,min},\ T_{prod}^{effect}\right\}
+T_{merge}+T_{safe},
$$

where

$$
T_{prod}^{effect}=\max\{T_{LC}^{prep}+T_{LC}^{exec},\ T_{speed}^{effect}\}.
$$

The horizon should also be shorter than the reliable HDV prediction horizon $H_{rel}$:

$$
H\le H_{rel}.
$$

The initial experiment should scan $H\in\{8,12,16\}$ s and use $H=12$ s as the main setting unless sensitivity results suggest otherwise.

## 4. Time-Expanded Merge Slots and Edge Validity

### 4.1 Candidate times and time-expanded slots

At time $t$, generate candidate merge times

$$
\mathcal T_H=\{\tau_k=t+k\Delta t_m:\ k=1,\ldots,K_H,\ \tau_k\le t+H\}.
$$

For each target-lane front-rear pair $(i,j)$ and each time $\tau_k$, define a time-expanded slot

$$
s=(i,j,k),\qquad t_s=\tau_k.
$$

The ramp-vehicle--slot edge is

$$
e=(r,i,j,k).
$$

This order is important: $\tau_k$ is generated before matching, and the matching variable selects which candidate time is used.

### 4.2 Feasible merge interval

At $\tau_k$, the feasible front-bumper interval for ramp vehicle $r$ is

$$
\mathcal I_{r,i,j,k}(\tau_k)=
\left[
 x_j(\tau_k)+L_r+d_{jr}^{rear}(\tau_k),\
 x_i(\tau_k)-L_i-d_{ri}^{front}(\tau_k)
\right].
$$

The interval width is

$$
W_{r,i,j,k}=x_i(\tau_k)-L_i-d_{ri}^{front}(\tau_k)-x_j(\tau_k)-L_r-d_{jr}^{rear}(\tau_k).
$$

A deterministic physical-validity declaration is

$$
V_{e}^{phys,pred}=\mathbf 1\{W_{r,i,j,k}\ge0\}.
$$

A simple dynamic safety-distance model is

$$
d_{ri}^{front}=d_0+T_{safe}v_r+\frac{[v_r-v_i]_+^2}{2b_{safe}},
$$

$$
d_{jr}^{rear}=d_0+T_{safe}v_j+\frac{[v_j-v_r]_+^2}{2b_{safe}}.
$$

This declaration is not a post-hoc emergency filter. It is the model's own statement that a candidate is physically valid or invalid under the predicted rollout.

### 4.3 Reachability

Define

$$
I_e^{reach}=1
$$

if there exists a ramp control trajectory $u_r(\cdot)$ such that the ramp vehicle reaches $\mathcal I_{r,i,j,k}(\tau_k)$ at $\tau_k$ while satisfying acceleration, jerk, speed, and ramp-end constraints. Otherwise $I_e^{reach}=0$.

A conservative screen without jerk is

$$
x_r^{min}(\tau_k)=x_r(t)+v_r(t)\Delta T+\frac12u_r^{min}\Delta T^2,
$$

$$
x_r^{max}(\tau_k)=x_r(t)+v_r(t)\Delta T+\frac12u_r^{max}\Delta T^2,
$$

where $\Delta T=\tau_k-t$. If

$$
[x_r^{min}(\tau_k),x_r^{max}(\tau_k)]\cap\mathcal I_{r,i,j,k}(\tau_k)\ne\emptyset,
$$

and the target speed is compatible with the local mainline speed, the edge passes the preliminary reachability screen. A jerk-constrained version can be implemented by discrete reachable-set propagation.

### 4.4 Sequential slot-validity events

Define the following events for edge $e=(r,i,j,k)$:

$$
\mathcal E_e^{surv}=\{\text{slot }(i,j,k)\text{ remains available until }\tau_k\},
$$

$$
\mathcal E_e^{safe}=\{\text{physical safety margins are nonnegative around execution}\},
$$

$$
\mathcal E_e^{rec}=\{\text{post-merge disturbance is recoverable within prescribed limits}\}.
$$

The recoverable valid probability is

$$
\boxed{
P_e^R=I_e^{reach}\,
P_e^{surv}\,
P_e^{safe\mid surv}\,
P_e^{rec\mid safe,surv}.
}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
P_e^R=I_e^{reach}
\mathbb P(\mathcal E_e^{surv}\mid\mathcal E_e^{reach})
\mathbb P(\mathcal E_e^{safe}\mid\mathcal E_e^{surv},\mathcal E_e^{reach})
\mathbb P(\mathcal E_e^{rec}\mid\mathcal E_e^{safe},\mathcal E_e^{surv},\mathcal E_e^{reach}).
$$

This is a standard chain-rule expression. It avoids assuming independence among survival, safety, and recoverability. The contribution is not the probability chain itself, but the use of sequential slot-validity events inside inventory matching and slot production.

For deterministic V0 simulation, use the cascade

$$
P_e^R=I_e^{reach}I_e^{surv}I_e^{safe}I_e^{rec}\in\{0,1\}.
$$

For stochastic IDM simulation, use scenario frequency

$$
\widehat P_e^R=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^NI_e^{reach,(n)}I_e^{surv,(n)}I_e^{safe,(n)}I_e^{rec,(n)}.
$$

### 4.5 Recovery debt

Recoverability should not be only a binary event. Define normalized recovery debt

$$
\boxed{
RD_e=
\beta_T\frac{T_{rec,e}}{\bar T_R}
+\beta_N\frac{N_{aff,e}}{\bar N}
+\beta_A\frac{A_{wave,e}}{\bar A}
+\beta_Q\frac{[\Delta q_{out,e}]_+}{\bar q}
+\beta_B\frac{[a_{rear,e}^{req}]_+}{\bar a_b}.
}
$$

Here $T_{rec}$ is local recovery time, $N_{aff}$ is affected vehicle count, $A_{wave}$ is local speed-wave amplitude, $\Delta q_{out}$ is downstream outflow loss, and $a_{rear}^{req}$ is required braking of the rear boundary vehicle or its follower. All terms are normalized and $\sum\beta=1$ by default.

The decision uses two layers:

1. $P_e^{rec\mid safe,surv}$ or $I_e^{rec}$ declares broad recoverability.
2. $RD_e$ compares the residual recovery burden among candidates.

This avoids double counting: $P_e^R$ determines whether an edge contributes to expected supply, while $RD_e$ measures the quality of the matched supply.

## 5. Inventory Supply, Diagnostic Matching, and Conflicts

### 5.1 Horizon demand

Let $\mathcal R_H$ be ramp vehicles expected to require merging within the horizon. Define demand weight

$$
\omega_r=1+\chi_w\frac{T_r^{wait}}{\bar T^{wait}+\epsilon}
+\chi_d\frac{[d_{crit}-d_{end,r}]_+}{d_{crit}+\epsilon},
$$

where $T_r^{wait}$ is waiting time and $d_{end,r}$ is distance to ramp end. Horizon demand is

$$
D_H=\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_H}\omega_r.
$$

### 5.2 Reservation-edge set

Not all geometric edges can be reserved. Under action $a$, define the reservation-edge set

$$
\mathcal E_{res}^a=\left\{e:
I_e^{reach,a}=1,
V_e^{phys,pred,a}=1,
P_e^{R,a}\ge p_{min},
P_e^{rec\mid safe,surv,a}\ge p_{rec}^{min},
RD_e^a\le RD_{max}
\right\}.
$$

If $e\notin\mathcal E_{res}^a$, then

$$
x_e^a=0.
$$

Thus a gap that is reachable but unsafe or high-debt is not silently assigned. It may, however, become a production target if it is a near-miss candidate.

### 5.3 Matching-consistent inventory supply

Let $x_e=x_{r,i,j,k}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether ramp vehicle $r$ is assigned to slot $(i,j,k)$. The recoverable inventory supply is

$$
\boxed{
S_H^{R,a}=\max_x\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{res}^a}\omega_rP_e^{R,a}x_e.
}
$$

Basic assignment constraints are

$$
\sum_{i,j,k}x_{r,i,j,k}\le1,\qquad\forall r,
$$

$$
\sum_r x_{r,i,j,k}\le1,\qquad\forall(i,j,k).
$$

The inventory deficit is

$$
\boxed{
Z_H^{R,a}=[D_H-S_H^{R,a}]_+.
}
$$

When $x_{r,i,j,k}=1$, the planned merge time of vehicle $r$ is

$$
t_r^{merge}=\tau_k.
$$

### 5.4 Slot-consumption conflict

Time-expanded copies of the same physical gap are not independent. If ramp vehicle $r_1$ consumes gap $(i,j)$ at $\tau_k$, the physical sequence changes and the same gap cannot simply be consumed again at $\tau_{k+1}$. Therefore Version 3.1 adds a conservative slot-consumption constraint:

$$
\sum_r\sum_{k\in\mathcal K_{ij}}x_{r,i,j,k}\le1,\qquad\forall(i,j),
$$

where $\mathcal K_{ij}$ denotes candidate times belonging to the same physical gap in the current rolling horizon. A less conservative version uses a conflict window:

$$
\sum_r\sum_{k':|\tau_{k'}-\tau_k|\le T_{consume}}x_{r,i,j,k'}\le1.
$$

More generally, define a conflict set $\mathcal C^a$ over edge pairs. Then

$$
x_e+x_{e'}\le1,
\qquad\forall(e,e')\in\mathcal C^a.
$$

The base model without conflict constraints is a bipartite matching. The conflict-aware model is a small rolling integer assignment. This is acceptable for the Python prototype because the algorithm evaluates only a limited top-$K$ set of candidate production actions and a short time horizon.

### 5.5 Diagnostic matching versus final reservation

The baseline matching under $a_0$ is a **diagnostic matching**, not a final commitment. It answers:

> Under natural evolution, how much ramp demand can be satisfied by currently valid recoverable slots?

If no natural edge satisfies the reservation conditions, then

$$
x^{diag,0}=0,
\qquad
S_H^{R,0}=0,
\qquad
Z_H^{R,0}=D_H.
$$

This is not a failure of the algorithm. It is a diagnosis that slot production may be needed. Final reservation is made only after the selected action $a^*$ is known and the action-conditioned slot set has been recomputed.

### 5.6 Demand-normalized recovery debt

For action comparison, use demand-normalized matched recovery debt:

$$
\boxed{
\bar{\mathcal D}_H^{R,a}=\frac{1}{D_H+\epsilon_D}
\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{res}^a}\omega_rP_e^{R,a}\widehat{RD}_e^a x_e^a.
}
$$

This is preferable to average debt per matched vehicle because an action that serves very few ramp vehicles should not appear good merely because its average debt is low. The inventory deficit term already penalizes unsatisfied demand, but demand-normalized debt keeps all RCMV terms on compatible units.

## 6. Near-Miss Slot Production and RCMV

### 6.1 When production is evaluated

Production actions are not generated in every state for every mainline gap. For computational efficiency and conceptual clarity, RPMI-CMV first solves baseline diagnostic matching. If

$$
Z_H^{R,0}\le\varepsilon_Z
\quad\text{and}\quad
\bar{\mathcal D}_H^{R,0}\le\varepsilon_D,
$$

then the algorithm uses natural reservation and no active production.

Otherwise, it constructs near-miss producible edges.

### 6.2 Near-miss producible edges

A near-miss edge is a ramp-aware candidate that is not currently a valid reservation but is close enough to validity that a small CAV action may convert it into one. Define

$$
\mathcal E_{prod}^{0}=\left\{e=(r,i,j,k):
I_e^{reach,0}=1,
 e\notin\mathcal E_{res}^{0},
\Delta W_e^{req}\le\bar W_{prod},
\text{at least one production mode is available}
\right\}.
$$

Here

$$
\Delta W_e^{req}=[W_{min}-W_e^0]_+
$$

or an equivalent safety-margin deficit measures how much improvement is needed to convert the edge into a valid slot.

This definition is central. The production target is not a ramp vehicle alone, nor a gap alone, but an edge $e=(r,i,j,k)$. It is gap-centric because it evaluates mainline disturbance around $(i,j)$; it is ramp-aware because reachability and demand weight depend on $r$ and $k$.

### 6.3 Candidate production actions

For each near-miss edge $e=(r,i,j,k)$, generate candidate actions according to boundary vehicle types and available upstream CAVs:

$$
\mathcal A_t(e)=\mathcal A_e^{LC}\cup\mathcal A_e^F\cup\mathcal A_e^R\cup\mathcal A_e^{FR}.
$$

The full candidate set is

$$
\mathcal A_t=\{a_0\}\cup\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal E_{prod}^{0,topK}}\mathcal A_t(e),
$$

where $\mathcal E_{prod}^{0,topK}$ is selected by a cheap screening score.

The boundary-type logic is:

| Front boundary $i$ | Rear boundary $j$ | Candidate speed-production action |
|---|---|---|
| CAV | HDV | front-boundary acceleration |
| HDV | CAV | rear-boundary deceleration |
| CAV | CAV | front-rear joint micro-adjustment |
| HDV | HDV | no active boundary speed production |

An HDV-HDV gap may still be a natural valid reservation edge. It simply cannot be actively widened by boundary speed control.

#### Front-boundary acceleration

If $i$ is a CAV, front-boundary acceleration uses

$$
0\le u_i(\tau)\le \bar a_{coop},\qquad \tau\in[t,\tau_k].
$$

The approximate gap increase is

$$
\Delta g_{ij}^{F}(\tau_k)=\int_t^{\tau_k}(\tau_k-\zeta)u_i(\zeta)d\zeta.
$$

#### Rear-boundary deceleration

If $j$ is a CAV, rear-boundary deceleration uses

$$
-\bar b_{coop}\le u_j(\tau)\le0,
\qquad \tau\in[t,\tau_k],
$$

and

$$
\Delta g_{ij}^{R}(\tau_k)=-\int_t^{\tau_k}(\tau_k-\zeta)u_j(\zeta)d\zeta.
$$

#### Front-rear joint micro-adjustment

If both boundary vehicles are CAVs, a small front-rear adjustment can distribute the required gap increase. The local generator minimizes a low-disturbance cost subject to CAV acceleration, jerk, and speed limits. This is only a candidate generator, not the main contribution.

#### Upstream lane-change production

An upstream lane-change action is

$$
a^{LC}=(c,\ell_o\rightarrow\ell_m,t_a),
$$

where $c$ is an outer-lane CAV and $\ell_m\in\mathcal L_i$ has sufficient receiving gap. In the first Python simulation, the lateral movement can be simplified as a lane-index transition over $T_{LC}$, while longitudinal states and surrounding-vehicle responses are explicitly rolled out.

### 6.4 Conservative action-conflict rule

Version 3.1 does not make multi-action bundles the main model. Instead, the conference-level implementation uses a single primary production action per control cycle, or a set of mutually non-overlapping actions as an optional extension.

A conservative controllability rule is:

> If a CAV is selected as an active boundary controller for one candidate slot, it is unavailable for active production of adjacent slots within the same control cycle.

This avoids the shared-boundary conflict in which a middle CAV would need to accelerate for one gap while decelerating for another. Full action-bundle scheduling, joint reservation, and ramp-platoon insertion are left for journal extension.

### 6.5 Cheap screening before rollout

To avoid evaluating all gaps, use a screening score:

$$
Score(e,m)=\omega_r\Delta \widehat P_e^R
+\lambda_D\Delta \widehat{RD}_e
-\lambda_C\underline C_{prod}(e,m),
$$

where $m\in\{F,R,FR,LC\}$ is a production mode. Only top-$K$ edge-mode pairs are evaluated by full action-conditioned rollout. The screening is not the final decision; it only controls computational load.

### 6.6 Action-conditioned rollout

For any candidate action $a\in\mathcal A_t$, perform a rollout:

$$
\widehat X_{\tau+\Delta t\mid t}^{a,\xi}
=F_\Delta\left(\widehat X_{\tau\mid t}^{a,\xi},u^{CAV}(\tau;a),\theta^{HDV},\eta_\tau\right).
$$

Then regenerate

$$
\mathcal S_H^a,
\quad
\mathcal E_{res}^a,
\quad
P_e^{R,a},
\quad
RD_e^a,
\quad
S_H^{R,a},
\quad
Z_H^{R,a}.
$$

The final matching under action $a$ is not forced to use the nominal edge that generated the action. If the rollout makes another edge more suitable, the action-conditioned matching may assign the slot to a different ramp vehicle. This preserves rolling reservation flexibility.

### 6.7 Normalized RCMV objective

Normalize the inventory deficit as

$$
\bar Z_H^{R,a}=\frac{Z_H^{R,a}}{D_H+\epsilon_D}.
$$

The one-step normalized objective is

$$
\boxed{
J(a)=\mathbb E[\bar Z_H^{R,a}]
+\lambda_D\mathbb E[\bar{\mathcal D}_H^{R,a}]
+\lambda_C\bar C_{prod}(a).
}
$$

Here $\bar C_{prod}(a)$ is the normalized mainline production cost:

$$
\bar C_{prod}(a)=\bar C_{LC}(a)+\bar C_{guide}(a)+\bar C_{neighbor}(a)+\bar C_{wave}(a).
$$

Ramp guidance effort is placed in the secondary reservation objective unless the evaluated action explicitly includes a ramp command. This avoids double-counting ramp consumption effort as mainline production cost.

The counterfactual marginal value is

$$
\boxed{
RCMV(a)=J(a_0)-J(a).
}
$$

Select

$$
a^*=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A_t}RCMV(a),
$$

and execute production only if

$$
RCMV(a^*)>\theta.
$$

If no action exceeds $\theta$, execute $a_0$ and use natural reservation or waiting.

## 7. Action-Conditioned Rolling Reservation Algorithm

### 7.1 Algorithm overview

At every control cycle:

1. Predict baseline trajectories under $a_0$.
2. Generate candidate times $\mathcal T_H$ and slots $\mathcal S_H^0$.
3. Form edges $e=(r,i,j,k)$ and compute reachability, validity, $P_e^R$, and $RD_e$.
4. Solve baseline diagnostic matching over $\mathcal E_{res}^0$ and obtain $x^{diag,0}$, $S_H^{R,0}$, $Z_H^{R,0}$, and $\bar{\mathcal D}_H^{R,0}$.
5. If baseline inventory is sufficient and debt is acceptable, set $a^*=a_0$.
6. Otherwise, identify near-miss producible edges $\mathcal E_{prod}^0$.
7. Generate candidate production actions from near-miss edges according to boundary vehicle types and available upstream CAVs.
8. Pre-screen actions and perform action-conditioned rollout for the top candidates.
9. For each action, regenerate slots, recompute edge qualities, and solve conflict-aware reservation.
10. Compute $RCMV(a)=J(a_0)-J(a)$ and select $a^*$.
11. If $RCMV(a^*)>\theta$, execute $a^*$; otherwise execute $a_0$.
12. Under the selected action, form final reservations and ramp speed guidance commands.
13. Advance the system without emergency fallback and record valid, invalid, unsafe, and recovery outcomes.

### 7.2 Pseudocode

```text
Input: state X_t, ramp set R_H, horizon H, thresholds p_min, RD_max, theta
Output: production action a*, reservations x*, ramp guidance plans

1: Roll out baseline trajectory under a0
2: Generate time grid T_H and slots S_H^0={(i,j,k)}
3: For each edge e=(r,i,j,k):
       compute I_reach^0, V_phys_pred^0, P_R^0, RD^0
4: Build reservation set E_res^0
5: Solve baseline diagnostic assignment over E_res^0
6: If Z_H^{R,0} <= eps_Z and Dbar_H^{R,0} <= eps_D:
       a* <- a0
       x* <- x^{diag,0}
       go to Step 15
7: Build near-miss production set E_prod^0
8: Generate candidate actions A_t from top-K near-miss edges
9: For each a in A_t:
       roll out X^a
       regenerate S_H^a and E_res^a
       compute P_R^a and RD^a
       solve conflict-aware reservation and compute J(a)
10: a* <- argmax_a [J(a0)-J(a)]
11: If RCMV(a*) <= theta:
       a* <- a0
12: Reuse or recompute reservation under selected a*
13: Generate ramp speed-guidance plan for matched ramp CAVs
14: Execute one control step without post-hoc safety fallback
15: Record realized invalidity, unsafe match, delay, debt, and disturbance metrics
```

### 7.3 Expiration and reassignment

A reserved edge expires if any of the following occurs before execution:

$$
I_e^{reach}=0,
\quad
V_e^{phys,pred}=0,
\quad
P_e^R<p_{min},
\quad
RD_e>RD_{max},
\quad
\tau_k<t.
$$

When expiration occurs, the affected ramp vehicle is returned to the demand set in the next rolling cycle. Local reassignment is performed using the latest state and time-expanded inventory. The algorithm does not force a ramp vehicle to chase an expired slot.

### 7.4 Physical validity declaration without fallback

The Python simulation distinguishes three concepts.

**Predicted candidate validity.** The method declares whether a candidate is valid before reservation:

$$
V_e^{phys,pred}=1\ \text{or}\ 0.
$$

**Decision use.** The proposed method reserves only edges declared valid under its prediction model. Invalid candidates may still be recorded as near-miss production targets if producible.

**Realized outcome label.** After executing the generated trajectory without fallback, the simulator records

$$
V_e^{phys,real}=1\ \text{or}\ 0,
$$

plus overlap, negative margin, unsafe match, collision-risk, hard-braking, and recovery labels.

No emergency controller repairs unsafe trajectories. If a candidate action or reservation leads to physical overlap in the no-fallback rollout, the outcome is recorded as a failure.

## 8. Theoretical Properties and Remarks

### Remark 1: The conditional probability chain is not a contribution claim

The expression for $P_e^R$ follows the standard product rule for sequential events. It is used to encode the semantics that a slot must first be reachable, then survive, then be safe, then be recoverable. The paper's contribution is the event design and its coupling with inventory, matching, and RCMV.

### Remark 2: Base matching versus conflict-aware assignment

If overlapping slot-consumption conflicts are ignored, the assignment is a maximum-weight bipartite matching between ramp vehicles and time-expanded slots, and the standard LP relaxation is integral. Once physical-gap consumption constraints or joint conflict constraints are added, the problem becomes a conflict-aware rolling integer assignment. Version 3.1 therefore does not claim integral relaxation for the full model.

### Proposition 1: RCMV is a one-step rollout objective improvement

Because

$$
RCMV(a)=J(a_0)-J(a),
$$

any selected action with $RCMV(a)>0$ improves the normalized one-step action-conditioned objective under the rollout model. This is not a closed-loop optimality guarantee. It is an interpretive property supporting the counterfactual comparison.

### Remark 3: Recovery debt is a proxy, not a universal traffic stability proof

Recovery debt summarizes local recovery time, affected vehicles, speed-wave amplitude, outflow loss, and braking burden. If local disturbances decay approximately as

$$
\|e(\tau)\|\le Ke^{-\alpha(\tau-\tau_k)}\|e(\tau_k^+)\|+\eta,
$$

then recovery time admits the bound

$$
T^{rec}\le\frac1\alpha\ln\left(\frac{K\|e(\tau_k^+)\|}{\varepsilon_R-\eta}\right),
\quad \eta<\varepsilon_R.
$$

This motivates recovery debt but should not be overstated as a guarantee for all mixed traffic states.

## 9. Python-First Numerical Experiment Design

### 9.1 Why Python first

The first validation stage uses a pure numerical Python simulator because it is transparent, controllable, easy to reproduce, and suitable for counterfactual rollout. It avoids hidden simulator safety fallback, lane-change fallback, and car-following repairs that could mask algorithmic failures. It also gives direct access to mechanism variables:

$$
G_H,
\quad S_H^p,
\quad S_H^R,
\quad Z_H^R,
\quad RD,
\quad RCMV.
$$

SUMO remains valuable for later external validation after the mechanism is verified.

### 9.2 Simulation phases

**V0: Deterministic mechanism simulation.**  
No HDV acceleration noise, no communication delay, no random cut-in, and no HDV lane changing. The purpose is to verify whether time-expanded inventory, near-miss production, RCMV comparison, and action-conditioned reservation work.

**V1: Stochastic IDM simulation.**  
Add HDV driver-type heterogeneity and acceleration noise. Estimate survival and safety by scenario frequency.

**V2: Extended uncertainty.**  
Add MOBIL-like HDV lane changes, random cut-ins, communication delay, and prediction error. These are robustness or journal-extension experiments.

### 9.3 Core scenarios

The main paper should not present too many scenarios as equally important. Version 3.1 recommends the following core set.

**S0: Mechanism-predictor experiment.**  
Randomly sample many traffic states and compare raw gap count, density imbalance, speed difference, perishable inventory, recoverable inventory, and $Z_H^R$ in predicting future 30 s ramp delay, slot expiration, hard braking, recovery time, and speed-wave amplitude.

**S1: Sparse mainline.**  
Mainline gaps are abundant. Expected result: all reasonable methods perform well; RPMI-CMV provides limited additional benefit. This prevents overclaiming.

**S2: Designed near-critical outer lane.**  
The outer lane is near critical loading, inner lane has spare capacity, ramp demand is medium-high, and CAV penetration is moderate. This is the primary mechanism scenario. RPMI-CMV should reduce $Z_H^R$, ramp waiting, slot expiration, recovery debt, hard braking, and speed-wave amplitude.

**S3: All-lanes congested.**  
Outer and inner lanes are both congested. Expected result: benefits are limited because low-disturbance slot production is infeasible. This is a failure condition, not a weakness to hide.

**S4: Low CAV penetration.**  
Few controllable boundary vehicles are available. Expected result: inventory diagnosis remains useful, but active production benefit decreases.

**S5: Boundary-control comparison.**  
Designed gaps include CAV-HDV, HDV-CAV, CAV-CAV, and HDV-HDV boundaries. Compare no production, lane-change production, front acceleration, rear deceleration, and joint micro-adjustment.

**S6: Raw-gap illusion.**  
Geometric gaps exist but are unreachable or high-debt. Expected result: raw gap reservation overestimates supply, while $S_H^R$ and $Z_H^R$ better predict merging difficulty.

**S7: Action-conditioned reservation ablation.**  
Compare reservation-before-production versus reservation-after-production. Expected result: action-conditioned reservation avoids stale or inconsistent assignments.

**S8: Recovery-debt trade-off.**  
Construct two candidate slots with similar safety but different post-merge recovery burden. Expected result: RPMI-CMV selects lower-debt slots even if raw geometric gap is smaller.

### 9.4 Metrics

Mechanism metrics:

$$
G_H,
\quad S_H^p,
\quad S_H^R,
\quad Z_H^R,
\quad RD,
\quad RCMV.
$$

Ramp-side metrics: waiting time, merge success rate, failed reservation rate, slot expiration, ramp acceleration cost, and speed-guidance magnitude.

Mainline-side metrics: average speed, speed variance, hard braking count, minimum gap, TTC, DRAC, local speed-wave amplitude, recovery time, affected vehicle count, and downstream outflow change.

Safety/failure labels: invalid slot count, unsafe match count, collision-risk event, physical overlap event, negative margin duration, and realized invalidity rate.

## 10. Baselines and Ablations

### 10.1 Core baselines

1. **Pure IDM baseline.** No coordination; ramp vehicles merge when natural acceptable gaps appear.
2. **FIFO merge reservation.** Ramp vehicles are served in arrival order with simple feasibility checks.
3. **Raw-gap reservation.** Reserves the largest or first geometric gap satisfying a size threshold.
4. **Speed-benefit lane change.** Mainline CAV lane changes are triggered by its own speed advantage.
5. **Density-balanced lane change.** Mainline CAV lane changes are triggered by lane density imbalance.
6. **RPMI-CMV.** Full proposed method with time-expanded inventory, near-miss production, RCMV, and action-conditioned reservation.

### 10.2 Required ablations

1. **Without perishability.** Set survival labels/probabilities to one.
2. **Without recoverability/debt.** Remove recovery event and recovery debt from matching and RCMV.
3. **Without RCMV.** Replace action selection by density or speed triggers.
4. **Without action-conditioned reservation.** Choose actions after baseline reservation and do not recompute matching.
5. **Lane-change-only RPMI.** Remove boundary speed production.
6. **Speed-production-only RPMI.** Remove upstream lane-change production.
7. **Without near-miss screening.** Evaluate all candidate gaps or use all raw gaps to show computational and decision noise.

### 10.3 Journal extension baselines

Journal-version comparisons can include CoMC-style flow-level gap creation, DCoMA-style dynamic cooperative merging assistance, dynamic-conflict-graph-style joint merging/lane-changing, and learned RCMV critic versus exact rollout. These should not dominate the conference-level mechanism paper.

## 11. Expected Results and Mechanism Validation

The expected evidence is not that RPMI-CMV is universally superior. The correct claim is conditional.

1. In sparse traffic, RPMI-CMV should provide limited benefit because natural gaps are abundant.
2. In all-lanes congestion, RPMI-CMV should also provide limited benefit because low-disturbance production is infeasible.
3. In the designed near-critical outer-lane scenario, RPMI-CMV should reduce recoverable deficit, ramp delay, slot expiration, recovery debt, hard braking, and speed-wave amplitude.
4. $Z_H^R$ should predict future merging difficulty better than raw gap count, density imbalance, or speed difference.
5. RCMV should choose different actions depending on boundary vehicle types and disturbance cost.
6. Action-conditioned reservation should reduce stale reservations and inconsistent assignments.
7. Recovery debt should reveal cases where a slot is geometrically safe but operationally harmful.

The paper should report negative or limited-benefit regimes explicitly. Doing so strengthens the credibility of the method.

---

## 12. Limitations and Extensions

First, the initial simulator does not include HDV active lane changing, random cut-ins, or communication delay. These are intentionally deferred to isolate the mechanism.

Second, the single-primary-action implementation is conservative. Full multi-action bundles, shared-boundary CAV scheduling, joint reservation, and ramp-platoon insertion are natural extensions but should not be made the core of Version 3.1.

Third, recovery debt weights are not universal constants. They should be normalized, reported, and tested through sensitivity analysis.

Fourth, exact rollout may be computationally expensive. A learned RCMV critic can be trained offline to pre-screen actions, while exact rollout remains the final verifier for the top candidates.

Fifth, the method targets a specific operating regime: near-critical outer-lane loading, inner-lane reserve capacity, medium-high ramp demand, and enough CAV penetration for limited low-disturbance production.

---

## 13. Conclusion

This paper develops RPMI-CMV, a recovery-debt-constrained perishable merge-slot inventory production and reservation method for mixed multi-lane on-ramp merging. Its central claim is not that CAVs can create gaps, change lanes, or guide ramp vehicles; these are established ideas. The central claim is that useful merge opportunities should be measured as pair-specific, time-expanded, perishable, recoverable, and matching-limited inventory.

Version 3.1 deliberately reduces formulaic complexity. It keeps the necessary structure: time-expanded candidate slots, edge-specific validity, recovery debt, matching-consistent supply, recoverable deficit, and counterfactual action value. It removes unnecessary overreach by treating action bundles, ramp platoon insertion, and full joint conflict scheduling as extensions. The main algorithm now follows an implementable sequence: baseline diagnostic matching, near-miss producible edge identification, low-disturbance action generation, action-conditioned rollout, conflict-aware reservation, and no-fallback outcome recording.

The next decisive step is not another purely textual iteration, but Python V0 implementation and mechanism experiments. The most important evidence should come from designed near-critical scenarios and mechanism-predictor tests showing that $Z_H^R$ and RCMV explain and improve future merging outcomes better than raw gap count, density imbalance, or speed-benefit triggers.
